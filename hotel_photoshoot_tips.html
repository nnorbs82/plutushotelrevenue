Hotel Photoshoot Tips:
How to Get the Best Shots

A complete practical guide to creating hotel images that are attractive, truthful and useful across the website, OTAs and marketing.

Hotel photography is often the first real experience a potential guest has with the property. Good images do more than prove that a room exists - they communicate scale, comfort, atmosphere, design and the type of stay a guest can expect. The strongest photos make the hotel feel understandable before the guest arrives.

A professional shoot therefore needs more than a list of rooms. It needs preparation, control of light, deliberate composition and a clear idea of what makes the hotel distinctive. The following guide keeps the practical structure from the original Plutus photography advice and updates it into a more commercial hotel perspective.

01

Scout the location in advance

Walk the hotel before the camera comes out. Check guest rooms, suites, lobby areas, restaurant, bar, meeting spaces, pool, wellness areas, gardens, terraces and the exterior. The purpose is to identify the features that deserve attention and to see where the light, angle or background may create problems.

Do not assume the best-looking room at midday will also be the best-looking room at 17:00. Note which windows face the sun, when views are clearest and which public areas are naturally busy. A short scouting pass can save a large part of the shoot from being spent solving preventable problems.

Practical tip: Take a few phone reference shots during the scout and note the ideal time for each important location.

02

Capture the hotel’s unique atmosphere

A five-star city hotel, an independent boutique property and a relaxed resort should not be photographed as if they were the same product. The shoot should make the hotel's identity visible. Luxury properties may rely on material quality, space, service details and calm. Boutique hotels often need personality, unusual furniture, art or small design moments. Resorts need to make leisure, outdoor space and the surrounding environment feel tangible.

Consistency matters. The image set should feel like one hotel, not a collection of different photographers, filters or moods. Editing, colour temperature and the balance between wide shots and details should support the actual brand rather than create a fictional one.

Practical tip: Ask one question before the shoot: what should a guest feel about this hotel after seeing only five images?

03

Use professional-grade equipment where it adds value

A modern phone can make an excellent supporting image, but a professional hotel shoot benefits from the control of a mirrorless or DSLR camera, quality lenses and a tripod. A wide-angle lens in roughly the 16-35mm range is useful for interiors, but it should be used carefully - exaggerated wide angles make a room look larger online and disappoint the guest later.

Prime or macro lenses can be useful for details such as food, materials and amenities. A sturdy tripod helps keep verticals clean and images sharp in lower light. Portable lights or reflectors can help in dark corners, but hotel photography normally looks more natural when supplementary light is soft rather than obvious.

Practical tip: The goal is not the widest possible room. It is an attractive image that still tells the truth about the space.

04

Master natural and artificial light

Light is one of the biggest differences between an ordinary hotel image and one that feels inviting. Open curtains and blinds, turn on the lamps that support the atmosphere, and watch for mixed colour temperatures. Natural daylight usually gives rooms their most believable sense of space, while exterior photography often benefits from the softer light around sunrise or sunset.

Artificial light requires control. Strong ceiling lights can create harsh shadows or yellow colour casts, while window light may be much cooler. A photographer can balance those sources in camera and in post-production, but the scene should still look like the hotel rather than a studio set.

Practical tip: Photograph important exterior and public spaces at more than one time of day when their atmosphere changes meaningfully.

05

Tell the guest journey, not only the architecture

A complete photo library should show what it feels like to move through the stay. The exterior and arrival set context. The lobby establishes the welcome. The room needs to communicate sleep, space, bathroom quality and view. Breakfast, restaurant, pool, terrace or wellness images then explain what happens beyond the bed.

Human presence can make those moments more immersive, but it does not always require full faces. A hand holding a drink, somebody reading near a window, a breakfast being served or luggage arriving at a room can suggest experience without turning the shoot into a lifestyle advertisement.

Practical tip: Plan the final image order before the shoot. If a critical part of the guest journey has no planned image, add it to the shot list.

06

Frame every shot with intention

Composition determines where the eye goes. Architectural lines, furniture and windows can guide attention toward the bed, a view or a strong feature. Symmetry works particularly well when the room design itself is symmetrical, while an off-centre composition can feel more natural in informal or boutique settings.

Decluttering is essential. Remove loose cables, bins, cleaning equipment, open cupboards, wrinkled linen, paper notices and anything else that pulls attention away from the product. Small problems become very visible once a photograph is enlarged on a booking site.

Practical tip: Straight vertical lines are particularly important in hotel interiors. Correct obvious perspective distortion rather than accepting leaning walls.

07

Give key amenities their own moment

Guests compare more than bedrooms. If the hotel has a pool, spa, rooftop, gym, restaurant, courtyard, kitchenette, meeting room or distinctive view, those features need photographs strong enough to stand on their own. A single distant image rarely communicates the value properly.

Combine wide views that explain the whole space with closer images that show texture and detail. Dining benefits from both the room atmosphere and the food. Wellness benefits from the facility as well as quieter sensory details. Outdoor areas often deserve both daytime and evening coverage.

Practical tip: Treat the features that justify the hotel's rate as hero content, not as afterthoughts at the end of the shoot.

08

Edit for consistency and professionalism

Post-production should improve clarity without changing the product. Exposure can be balanced, highlights recovered, shadows opened and colours corrected, but the room should still look like the room. Oversaturated greenery, unreal skies and extreme HDR may initially look dramatic but reduce trust when the guest arrives.

A consistent edit across the library makes the brand feel intentional. Presets can help create consistency, but every image still needs individual checking for white balance, straight lines, dust, reflections and small visual distractions.

Practical tip: Before publishing, compare edited images side by side. The set should feel coherent even when different spaces have very different lighting.

09

Do not forget the hotel after dark

Some hotels become more attractive in the evening. Façade lighting, bars, restaurants, terraces, city views and pools can look entirely different after sunset. Night photography is especially useful when evening atmosphere is part of what the guest is buying.

A tripod, lower ISO and longer exposures usually create cleaner results than pushing the camera to very high sensitivity. The photographer should also protect highlights from lamps and signage so that the image retains detail rather than becoming a collection of bright spots.

Practical tip: If the hotel sells an evening experience, show it. A daylight-only library can leave a large part of the product invisible.

10

Shoot RAW for the important work

RAW files retain much more image information than compressed JPEG files. That gives the photographer greater flexibility to recover highlights, lift shadows and correct white balance - especially useful in hotel rooms where bright windows and darker interiors appear in the same frame.

The final files delivered to the website and OTAs will normally be compressed formats, but capturing the original in RAW creates a better master library and more room for future re-editing.

Practical tip: Keep organised master files and export separate web-ready versions rather than repeatedly recompressing the same JPEG.

11

Protect usage rights and guest privacy

A hotel should know exactly where it is allowed to use the images. The agreement with a professional photographer should define usage rights clearly, including the hotel website, OTAs, social media, paid advertising, press and any limitations on duration or territory.

Guests or models who are identifiable in marketing images should have the appropriate permission. The same care applies to artwork, licensed content and third-party material visible in a campaign. A strong photo library is an asset only when the hotel can legally use it where it needs it.

Practical tip: Keep the photographer agreement, releases and original files together with the image library so future teams know what can be used.

The best hotel photography makes the guest want the stay - without making the real stay feel like a downgrade.
