01 / BUSINESS MIX

Not all occupancy has the same value.

The right mix depends on rate, booking behaviour, cancellation risk, acquisition cost, displacement and what each segment contributes over time. A full hotel can still be commercially weak if the wrong business occupied the inventory.

  • Balance direct, OTA, corporate, groups, wholesale and tour-operator demand.
  • Identify under-performing segments and realistic ways to improve penetration.
  • Create group quotation guidelines and review important opportunities individually.
  • Protect high-demand dates around events, fairs and market compression.
  • Review contracted rates and distribution partners by actual contribution.
  • Look for lower-cost ways to deliver demand when existing channels are expensive.
The target is not maximum occupancy. It is the strongest mix of demand the hotel can realistically attract and serve.
02 / YIELD & PRICING

Price the inventory that is still left to sell.

Rate should move with pressure, remaining inventory, booking window and room-type demand rather than with a static calendar. Strong pricing protects value when demand is present and creates a reason to book when it is not.

  • Competitor benchmarking and market-position review.
  • Dynamic pricing using pickup, pace and remaining inventory.
  • Restrictions and targeted promotions for strong and weak periods.
  • Room-type yield management and category differentials.
  • Length-of-stay controls where the demand pattern justifies them.
  • Daily pickup analysis with regular strategy adjustment.
A strong rate decision is not simply higher or lower. It is the rate and condition most likely to maximise the value of the remaining inventory.
03 / FORECASTING

A useful forecast creates time.

Forecasting is not an exercise in predicting the future perfectly. It is a way to see strong dates early enough to protect them and weak periods early enough to do something smarter than a last-minute blanket discount.

  • Historical and advanced pickup models where the data supports them.
  • Daily and weekly pace monitoring against previous periods, budget and target.
  • Monitoring citywide sell-out dates, events and unusual demand spikes.
  • Rolling rooms-revenue forecast for the current and forward months.
  • Forecasted occupancy, ADR and revenue with variance to budget and last year.
  • Monthly performance analysis with explanation of the drivers behind the result.
Forecasting is useful when it changes a decision before the final result arrives.
04 / DISTRIBUTION

Gross bookings can hide weak net value.

Each channel has a different audience, cost, promotion structure and ability to create incremental demand. Distribution therefore needs a revenue view, not only a connectivity view.

  • Review OTA visibility and promotions against a defined commercial need.
  • Improve OTA content, room mapping, rate plans and merchandising.
  • Balance direct and indirect demand instead of treating them as opposing sides.
  • Identify leakage, mapping problems and avoidable rate inconsistencies.
  • Check that PMS, channel manager and booking engine are configured correctly.
  • Measure commission and promotional dilution so channel performance is understood net of cost.
A channel can look powerful in gross revenue and weak in net contribution. The hotel needs both views.
05 / PERFORMANCE

Reporting should end with a next move.

Performance data matters when it explains what changed and what the hotel should do next. The goal is not a larger report - it is a shorter list of better priorities.

  • Occupancy, ADR, RevPAR and rooms-revenue performance.
  • Pickup and pace by stay date, booking window and room category.
  • Business mix and channel contribution.
  • Comparison to last year, same-day-last-year, forecast and budget.
  • Net ADR and acquisition-cost considerations where channel cost matters.
  • Identify dates to protect, dates to stimulate and issues that need attention.
Being ahead of last year does not automatically mean the hotel is performing well. Every result needs context.

FAQ

Revenue management without the jargon.

What is revenue management?

Revenue management uses demand data, booking behaviour, market context and commercial controls to make better decisions about pricing, availability and distribution. In a hotel, the inventory expires every night, so timing matters.

Can remote revenue management work as well as on-site?

Yes, provided the revenue manager has reliable system access, trustworthy data and close communication with the property. Most core revenue work happens through the PMS, distribution systems, market information and regular contact with the hotel team.

What are the main benefits?

More structured pricing, earlier identification of demand changes, stronger forecasting, better control of distribution cost and a clearer understanding of which commercial actions are actually improving performance.

Does revenue management mean constantly raising prices?

No. It also means knowing when the hotel is overpriced, when demand needs stimulation, when a restriction should be removed and when protecting rate is more valuable than chasing occupancy.
